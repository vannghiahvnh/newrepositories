SLEEPY SANGHA
"METTA — THE KINDNESS YOU FORGOT TO GIVE YOURSELF"
Full Sleep Script | Approx. 3 Hours

SECTION 1 — WELCOME (approx. 2-3 minutes)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Wherever you are right now...

however this day found you...

you are welcome here.

You can set down whatever you have been carrying.
The decisions.
The questions.
The things that were left unsaid.

None of them need to be solved tonight.

Tonight, there is nothing you need to do.
There is nowhere you need to be.
There is no version of yourself you need to become.

You are already here.

And here... is enough.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

Before we begin, feel free to share what time it is
and where you are listening from.

Tonight we are going to spend time with one of the most
beautiful teachings in all of Buddhism.

A practice so simple...
it can be held in a single breath.

And yet so deep...
that monks have devoted entire lifetimes to its exploration.

It is called Metta.

In the ancient Pali language of the Buddha's time,
Metta means loving-kindness.

Goodwill.
A warmth that wishes well.

Not the love that depends on being loved in return.
Not the love that comes and goes with mood or circumstance.

But a steady, quiet, unshakeable wish...

that all beings — including you —
may be happy.
May be at peace.
May be free from suffering.

Tonight, we are going to walk through the Metta teachings together.
We will rest in stories.
We will sit with ancient wisdom.

And slowly, gently...
we will let this kindness move through us
like warm water moving through cold hands.

You do not need to know anything about Buddhism.
You do not need to believe anything in particular.

The only invitation tonight is this:

to listen...
to let the words land...
and to see what is true in your own experience.

Take a slow, deep breath.

Wherever you are, let yourself settle in.

Thank you for joining us here at Sleepy Sangha.

Let us begin.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
SECTION 2 — THEMATIC SETUP (approx. 3 minutes)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

There is something I want to ask you...
before we go any further.

There is no right answer.
There is only the honest one.

When was the last time...
you were truly kind to yourself?

Not productive.
Not disciplined.
Not just "getting through the day."

But genuinely, warmly, quietly kind.

The way you might be to a dear friend
who called you late at night
and said they were struggling.

Most of us find this... surprisingly difficult.

We offer patience to strangers.
We forgive friends for mistakes
we would never forgive in ourselves.

We hold the door for people we will never see again.
We send messages to check in on others.
We ask if people are doing all right.

And yet...

when we lie down at night...
when the room goes quiet and the day falls away...

we speak to ourselves in ways
we would never speak to someone we loved.

We replay the moment we said the wrong thing.
We remind ourselves of the ways we fell short.
We add up what we did not finish.
We wonder if we are enough.

The Buddha noticed this.

He noticed that human beings are often
simultaneously too hard on themselves
and too afraid to look clearly at the stories they are living by.

And so he offered a practice.

Not a philosophy to argue about.
Not a doctrine to believe in.

But something you can do... right now... tonight...
in the quiet of this room.

He called it Metta Bhavana.

The cultivation of loving-kindness.

And the very first direction he pointed...
was not outward.

It was here.

Inward.

Toward you.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
CYCLE 1 — YOURSELF FIRST (approx. 30 minutes)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

[THE TEACHING]

There is a verse from the Metta Sutta —
one of the oldest texts in all of Buddhism.

The Buddha is said to have taught it to a group of monks
who were afraid of the spirits in the forest grove
where they had gone to meditate.

He told them:

"Whatever living beings there may be —
without exception...
whether they are weak or strong,
long or large,
medium, short, small, or fat,
seen or unseen,
those who dwell near and those who dwell far —

may all beings, without exception, be happy."

And then he said something the monks did not expect.

He said: begin with yourself.

Because how can you genuinely wish happiness for others...
if you have never learned to wish it for yourself?

How can you offer water from a well
you have never learned to drink from?

The practice of Metta begins with four simple words.
Perhaps the simplest words in all of Buddhist teaching:

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease.

Four small wishes.
Directed inward.

Not as a performance.
Not as something you must believe before you say it.

But as a genuine offering...
to the person who has been with you
every single moment of your life.

Tonight, before we go any further,
I want you to simply hear those words.

You don't need to feel them yet.
You don't need to believe them.

Just let them land...
like leaves falling onto still water.

May I be happy.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

May I be healthy.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

May I be safe.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

May I live with ease.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


[STORY 1 — THE HEALER WHO FORGOT TO HEAL HERSELF]

Let us rest in a story.

There was once a healer
who lived in a small village at the edge of a wide river.

She had learned her craft from her grandmother,
and her grandmother's grandmother before that.

She knew which roots to crush for fevers.
Which leaves to steep for sorrow.
Which teas to brew for the ones who came to her door
in the long, sleepless middle of the night.

People came from the next village, and the village beyond that.
They came with aching backs and broken hearts.
They came with children who would not eat
and elders who could not rest.

And she helped them.
Every one.

She was known as the kindest woman in the valley.
Someone who always had time.
Always had tea.
Always had the right quiet thing to say
when words were needed...
and nothing to say when silence was better.

But one evening, her oldest friend came to visit.

She found the healer sitting alone at her table.
The lamp burned low.
She was surrounded by her medicines and her herbs
and her books of remedies.

Her friend sat across from her
and looked at her for a long time without speaking.

Finally she said:

"When did you last sleep a full night?"

The healer looked up.
She tried to remember.
She could not.

"When did you last eat a meal
without thinking about someone else's hunger?"

The healer was quiet.

"When did you last sit —
the way you help others sit —
and simply let yourself rest?"

The healer said nothing.
Her eyes filled.

Her friend reached across the table
and took her hands.

"You know every remedy for every kind of suffering,"
she said gently.

"Except perhaps... this one."

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

The healer looked down at their joined hands.

"Which one?" she whispered.

"The suffering that comes from forgetting to include yourself."

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]

The healer did not speak for a long time.

When she finally looked up,
there were tears on her face.

But her expression was not grief exactly.

It was something softer.
Something that looked a little like recognition.

"I know how to be kind to everyone," she said at last.

"I have just... forgotten to practice it here."

She touched her own chest.
Gently.
As if for the first time.

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]


[REFLECTION 1]

There is something in this story
that many of us know without admitting.

We become very skilled at caring for others.
We learn to read the room.
To notice the heaviness in a friend's voice.
To offer exactly the right kind of attention.

And somewhere in the middle of all that giving...
we forget to turn the same gaze inward.

Not from selfishness.
Not from weakness.

From a simple, quiet misunderstanding —

that kindness directed at yourself
is somehow less important
than kindness directed at others.

The Buddha taught that this is not true.

The Metta Sutta does not say:
begin with your neighbor and work your way inward.

It says: begin here.
Begin with the being you know most intimately.
Begin with the one who has never once left your side.

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


[STORY 2 — THE OLD MONK AND THE MIRROR]

There is a story told of an old monk
who had spent forty years in practice.

He had meditated in forests and on mountainsides.
He had sat with teachers of great wisdom.
He had read the ancient texts
until the pages softened and the ink grew thin with use.

One of his students came to him one afternoon and asked:

"Teacher... in all your years of practice,
what has been the most difficult thing?"

The old monk was quiet for a very long time.

Then he said:

"Once, in my thirtieth year of practice,
I found an old mirror
in the corner of a storage room.

And I sat before it...
and tried to do what I had spent thirty years
telling others to do."

"What was that?" the student asked.

"I tried," the monk said slowly,
"to look at the face in the mirror...
the way I would look at someone I loved."

The student waited.

"It took me three days," the monk said.

"Three full days...
before I could do it
without looking away."

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]

The student did not fully understand at the time.

But years later,
sitting alone in her own practice,
trying to hold the simple words —

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease —

she understood exactly what the old monk had meant.

There is resistance, for many of us, in those words.

A feeling that the "I" in "may I be happy"
is somehow smaller...
less deserving...
than the "you" in "may you be happy."

A quiet voice that says:
yes, happiness for others.
But for me?
I still have work to do first.
I still need to become better first.
I still need to earn it first.

The Buddha called this a misunderstanding.

Not a moral failing.
Just a misunderstanding.

Because loving-kindness is not a reward for being good enough.

It is the beginning.

It is the ground from which everything else grows.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

Tonight, as you rest here,
I want to invite you —
not to force warmth where there is none,
not to pretend to feel what you do not feel —

but simply to offer these words toward yourself.

Quietly.
Without effort.

Like setting a candle in a window.
Not demanding it light the room.
Just... setting it there.

May I be happy.

[PAUSE — 4 seconds]

May I be healthy.

[PAUSE — 4 seconds]

May I be safe.

[PAUSE — 4 seconds]

May I live with ease.

[PAUSE — 10 seconds]

CYCLE 2 — THE BELOVED (approx. 30 minutes)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

[THE TEACHING]

In the traditional Metta practice,
after we turn kindness toward ourselves,
we extend it outward —

in widening circles,
like ripples moving across still water.

The first circle beyond ourselves is the easiest.

The beloved.

Someone whose happiness, when you think of it,
rises in you naturally.

A child.
A parent.
An old friend.
A teacher.
A pet who waits for you by the door.

The Buddha recognized this love.
He did not dismiss it.

But he offered a teaching about it
that many people find surprising.

When we send loving-kindness to someone we love deeply...
we sometimes notice that beneath the warmth...
there is also fear.

Fear that they might suffer.
That they might leave.
That something will happen to them
that we cannot prevent.

The Buddha taught that this fear is not Metta.

Metta is not the love that grips.
It is the love that releases.

Not: "I want you to be happy
because your happiness keeps me safe."

But: "May you be happy —
simply because you are alive...
and because you deserve to be."

The verse the Buddha offered for this direction is this:

"As a mother would protect her only child with her own life,
even so let one cultivate a boundless love
toward all beings."

A mother's love — at its purest — is not possessive.

A mother who truly loves her child
wants that child to be free.
To find their own path.
To make their own mistakes.
To live fully.

Metta, like that kind of love,
does not ask anything in return.

It only wishes well.

May you be happy.
May you be healthy.
May you be safe.
May you live with ease.

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]


[STORY 3 — THE FATHER AT THE RIVER]

Let us rest in a story.

There was a man who had a daughter who loved the river.

From the time she could walk,
she had been drawn to the water —
the way it moved,
the sounds it made,
the way it caught the light in the early morning
when the rest of the world was still sleeping.

By the time she was a child, she could swim further
than any of her friends.

By the time she was a young woman,
she had crossed rivers
he would not have dared to cross himself.

He worried about her.
Every crossing.
Every morning she went to the water before he woke.

One evening he went to his teacher —
an old woman who had lived by that same river
her entire life —

and he said:

"I love my daughter
more than I have loved anything in this world.
But the love terrifies me.
There is always this feeling underneath it —
that I could lose her.
How do I hold so much love
without being crushed by the fear?"

The teacher was quiet.

Then she said:

"Tell me. What does the river do
when your daughter swims in it?"

He blinked.
"It... carries her."

"And what does the river do
when she reaches the other side
and climbs out?"

He thought.
"It... keeps moving."

The teacher nodded slowly.

"Metta," she said,
"is not the love that holds on.
It is the love that carries...
and then keeps moving."

She looked at him gently.

"Your love for your daughter
does not have to grip her to be real.

It can carry her safely to the other side.

And when she is there,
swimming in rivers you have never seen...
that love can still be there.

Not as fear.

As a wish."

She paused.

"May you be happy.
May you be safe.
May you live with ease."

"That is the whole of it," she said.

"That is enough."

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


[REFLECTION 2]

There is something that shifts
when we learn to hold love this way.

Not as a grip.
As a wish.

When we love someone and we are afraid —
the fear comes from holding on too tight.
From needing the outcome to be a certain way.

Metta is the practice of loving fully...
and releasing fully...
at the same time.

May you be happy.
May you be healthy.
May you be safe.
May you live with ease.

These words carry no condition.
They ask for nothing.
They need nothing in return.

They are simply...
good wishes, sent outward.

Like a light left on for someone
who may or may not be coming home.

The light does not ask to be noticed.
It simply shines.

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]


[STORY 4 — THE LETTER THAT WAS NEVER SENT]

There is a story of two sisters
who had not spoken to each other for many years.

No one remembered exactly when the silence began.

As with many silences,
it had started small —
a misunderstanding,
a word taken wrongly,
a day when someone did not call when they should have —

and it had grown, quietly,
the way things grow when left in the dark.

The older sister, now in her fifties,
thought about her younger sister often.

She thought about her when she heard a particular song
they had both loved as children.

She thought about her when she saw two little girls
running together in a park.

She thought about her on birthdays
and on difficult days
and on ordinary Tuesdays
when nothing in particular happened.

She never stopped loving her.

She had simply also never found a way back.

One evening, she sat down and began to write a letter.

She wrote everything —
the misunderstanding,
the pride that had kept her from reaching out,
the years of missing her.

She wrote it all.

And then she put the letter in an envelope.

And she sat with it for a long time.

She knew she might not be able to send it.
She was not sure her sister would want to hear from her.
She did not know what the reply would be,
or if there would be one.

But before she put the letter away,
she did something she had not done in a very long time.

She whispered the words her own teacher had taught her,
many years ago:

May you be happy.
May you be healthy.
May you be safe.
May you live with ease.

Not because she knew her sister was happy.
Not because she could ensure her sister's safety.

But because those wishes were still alive in her.

They had never stopped being alive.

The letter was sent three days later.

The reply came a week after that.

It was short. Just a few lines.

But it was a beginning.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


[REFLECTION 3]

Metta does not always produce dramatic reunions.

Sometimes it simply...
clears a small space inside us.

A space where the wish still lives.

Even when the relationship is complicated.
Even when we don't know the outcome.
Even when we are still afraid.

May you be happy.
May you be healthy.
May you be safe.
May you live with ease.

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]

Notice how you are feeling.

There is no right way to feel.

Take a slow breath in.

Feel the weight of your body.
The surface beneath you.
The quiet that is here, in this room, right now.

CYCLE 3 — THE NEUTRAL PERSON (approx. 28 minutes)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

[THE TEACHING]

The third direction of Metta is the most quietly challenging.

Not the difficult person —
we will come to them.

But the neutral person.

The stranger on the train.
The neighbor you have passed for seven years
without ever learning their name.

The person who handed you your coffee this morning.
The woman in the shop.
The man walking his dog at dusk.

We tend not to think about neutral people.

They live at the edges of our awareness —
present, but not noticed.

And yet the Buddha considered this practice
to be one of the most important expansions of the heart.

Because when we limit our goodwill
to the people we know and love,
we have not yet fully understood what goodwill is.

Metta — true Metta —
is not personal.

It does not depend on knowing someone's name,
or their history,
or whether they have ever done anything for us.

It rests on one single recognition:

This person, whoever they are,
wants to be happy.

Just like you.

They want to be free from suffering.

Just like you.

They carry something within them that aches and hopes and tries.

Just like you.

The Buddha called this the "just like me" recognition —
the seed of compassion.

Once you see it...
once you really see it...

it becomes very difficult to walk past people
without something in you softening.

May you be happy.
May you be healthy.
May you be safe.
May you live with ease.

Sent to no one in particular.
And everyone at once.

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]


[STORY 5 — THE YOUNG MAN ON THE BUS]

Let us rest in a story.

A young man was riding a bus in the early morning,
going to work.

He was tired.
He had not slept well.

He had a meeting he was not looking forward to
and a problem he had not yet solved
and a list of things he needed to do
that was longer than the time he had to do them.

He sat by the window and looked out.

Across the aisle from him sat a woman he had never seen before.

She was perhaps sixty.
She wore a light blue coat.
She had a canvas bag on her lap.
She was looking out her own window.

That was all he knew about her.

And yet — something caught him that morning.
Something he would not be able to explain afterward.

He found himself wondering about her.

What was she going home to?
Or going toward?

Had she slept?
Did she have someone waiting for her?

What was the longest journey she had ever made,
and had it been worth it?

He thought:

I have no idea who this person is.
I will probably never see her again.

But she is sitting right here.
She has lived an entire life I know nothing about.

And very quietly, without deciding to,
he thought:

I hope she's okay.

I hope today is a gentle one for her.

I hope she has someone who sees her.

He did not know the word Metta.
He had never studied Buddhism.

But for a moment,
on that bus,
in the early grey morning light —

his heart had done something ancient and simple.

It had extended a wish toward a stranger.

Just because she was there.
Just because she was human.
Just because she, like him,
was doing her best.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


[REFLECTION 4]

Something opens in us
when we practice seeing the neutral people.

The difficult person.

Someone who has hurt you.
Disappointed you.

The Buddha did not ask us to pretend

He did not ask us to say

He did not ask us to rush toward forgiveness
before we are ready.

Holding hatred is like drinking poison
and waiting for the other person to fall ill.

Resentment lives in us.
Not in them.

It burns in our chest, not in theirs.
It interrupts our sleep, not theirs.

And so the practice —
this difficult, brave, quiet practice —

is not for them.

It is for you.

Not to excuse what they did.
Not to pretend the wound did not happen.

But to stop carrying the wound
as if it defines you.

To stop organizing your inner world
around someone who may not even know
they hurt you.

The Buddha's guidance was this:

Begin small.
Begin with someone only slightly difficult.

A stranger who was rude.
A colleague who was dismissive.
A person who cut you off in traffic.

And say, slowly, honestly,

May you be free from suffering.
May you find peace.

Not because you feel it yet.

But as a practice.
As an intention.

As the first tiny turn
away from resentment
and toward open air.

She had never thought that before.

Not forgiveness exactly.

A tiny opening.

May you find peace.

Not yet.

Just slightly.
Just for now.

The tempter.

Mara sent his armies.

and the Buddha sat.

What gives you the right?

He did not argue.
He did not raise his voice.

He reached down and touched the earth.
The earth itself bore witness.

And then he looked at Mara —
this figure of fear and illusion —

and what he felt...
was not contempt.

What he felt... was compassion.

Because Mara, too, was suffering.
Mara, too, was caught in a story he could not see past.

when they do not know another way.

Later, in many stories,
when Mara returned —
as he did, again and again —

the Buddha would simply say:

"I see you, Mara."

And in that seeing —
with no hatred,
no fear,
only clear-eyed, compassionate seeing —

Mara had no power.
