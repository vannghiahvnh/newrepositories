Wherever you are right now...

however this day found you...

you are welcome here.

You can set down whatever you have been carrying tonight.
The decisions that didn't get made.
The conversations that went a different direction than you hoped.
The small moments where you wished you had said something different,
or said nothing at all.

All of it can wait until morning.

None of it needs to be solved right now.

Tonight, there is only this.

The quiet.
The dark.
The sound of your own breathing.
And the knowledge that you made it here,
to the end of another day,
which is no small thing.

[PAUSE — 4 seconds]

Before we begin, feel free to share what time it is
and where in the world you are listening from tonight.
There are people settling in all across the world right now —
in different time zones, different rooms, different seasons —
and somehow, through this small window,
we find ourselves in the same quiet together.

I find that remarkable.

Tonight we are going to spend time with one of the most beautiful
and least understood teachings in all of Buddhism.

A practice that has been passed down for more than 2,500 years.
A practice so ancient that the language it was first taught in
no longer exists anywhere in the world
except in these texts.

And yet...
the practice itself is so simple
you could hold it in a single breath.

It is called Metta.

In the ancient Pali language of the Buddha's time,
Metta means loving-kindness.

Goodwill.

A warm, steady wish for the wellbeing of others.

And — perhaps most importantly, most surprisingly,
most countercultural for those of us raised to put others first —

a warm, steady wish for the wellbeing of yourself.

Not the love that depends on being loved in return.
Not the love that comes and goes with how the day went.
Not the love that says: I will be kind to you
once you have given me a reason.

But a love that simply... is.

That does not require the other person to be good,
or to be grateful,
or to be anything in particular.

That wishes well —
the way the sun warms everything it touches
without asking whether the thing deserves warmth.

Tonight, we are going to walk through the Metta teachings together.

We will rest in stories from ancient traditions and quiet corners of the world.
We will sit with wisdom that has survived thousands of years
because it keeps turning out to be true.

And slowly...
very slowly...
over the course of this long, quiet evening together...

we will let this kindness move through us
the way warmth moves through cold hands held near a fire.

You do not need to know anything about Buddhism.
You do not need to believe anything in particular.
You do not need to be in any special state of mind.

The only invitation tonight is this:

to listen...
to let the words come and go like breath...
and to notice, without forcing anything,
what is already true in your own experience.

Take a slow, deep breath.

Let your shoulders fall.
Let your jaw soften.
Let your hands rest open.

Thank you for joining us here at Sleepy Sangha.

Let us begin.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
THEMATIC SETUP
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

There is a question I want to sit with for a moment before we go any further.

Not a question you need to answer out loud.
Not a question with a right or wrong answer.

Just a question to let rest in your chest
like a small stone dropped into still water.

When was the last time you were genuinely kind to yourself?

Not productive.
Not disciplined.
Not efficient or organized or on top of things.

But actually, warmly, quietly kind.

The kind of kind you might be
to a dear friend who called you late on a Tuesday night
and said they were struggling.

When that friend calls, you don't assess them.
You don't give them a list of the ways they could improve.
You don't remind them of all the times they have failed
at the very things they are struggling with now.

You just... listen.
You make the space bigger, not smaller.
You say: of course you feel that way.
I'm here.
You are not alone.

And then you stay on the phone.

Most of us find it surprisingly easy to be that person for others.

And surprisingly, painfully difficult to be that person for ourselves.

We offer patience to strangers without thinking about it.
We hold the elevator door for people we will never see again.
We give our seat on the bus.
We check in on friends even when our own lives are heavy.
We write the birthday message, make the extra effort,
stay a little longer than we needed to.

And then we lie down at night —
in a room that is quiet,
in a body that is tired —

and we speak to ourselves in a voice we would never use
with someone we loved.

We replay the meeting where we said the wrong thing.
We add up everything we didn't finish.
We remind ourselves of the way we looked in that photo,
or the way we sounded on that call,
or the way we handled that situation three years ago
that we still somehow haven't forgiven ourselves for.

The Buddha noticed this.

He spent years watching the human mind —
his own mind, first, and then the minds of his students —
and one of the things he noticed most clearly
was that people are almost universally harder on themselves
than they are on anyone else in their lives.

And that this harshness...
this relentless internal critic
that most of us carry so quietly we have forgotten it is there...

is one of the deepest sources of suffering.

Not the circumstances of our lives.
Not the difficult people.
Not the things that go wrong.

But the voice inside that says: and it's your fault.
And you should have known better.
And other people don't struggle with this.
And you are behind, falling short, not enough.

That voice.

The Buddha did not ask us to silence that voice through willpower.
He did not say: just think more positive thoughts.
He did not offer a system of self-improvement
designed to eventually make us worthy of our own kindness.

He offered something much simpler.
And much more radical.

He said: practice.

Practice turning kindness inward,
the same way you would practice anything —
not because you feel it naturally and effortlessly,
but because the feeling follows the practice,
not the other way around.

He called it Metta Bhavana.

The cultivation of loving-kindness.

And the very first direction he pointed...
was not outward toward others.

It was inward.
Toward you.
Toward the one you have been neglecting
all this time,
while you were so busy taking care of everyone else.

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]

CYCLE 1 — YOURSELF FIRST
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

[THE TEACHING]

There is a verse from the Metta Sutta —
one of the oldest surviving texts in all of Buddhist literature.

Scholars believe it was written down sometime in the third century
before the common era,
though the teaching itself is believed to be older still —
passed from teacher to student by word of mouth
for generations before anyone thought to put it on paper.

The story behind the text is this.

A group of monks had gone into the forest to meditate.
They had found a beautiful grove, quiet and cool,
with tall trees and clean water and good ground for sitting.

They were pleased with it.

But after a few days...
they began to feel unsettled.

Not because anything was visibly wrong.
Not because any person or creature had threatened them.

But because of a feeling.

A creeping, nameless unease.
A sense that something in the grove was not welcoming them.
A feeling that something was watching.

They went back to the Buddha and told him what they had experienced.
They asked if they should find a different place.

And the Buddha said: no.
Go back to the grove.

But before you go...
let me teach you something to take with you.

And he taught them Metta.

He said: as you sit in that grove tonight...
as the darkness comes and the sounds of the forest change...
as your mind begins to fill with whatever it fills with
when you are quiet and alone...

do this.

Let your heart open in all directions.

Let it send warmth outward —
to the east and to the west,
to the north and the south,
above and below and all around —
without limits.
Without exceptions.

And he gave them the words:

Whatever living beings there may be —
without exception,
whether they are weak or strong,
long or large,
medium, short, small, or fat,
seen or unseen,
those who dwell near and those who dwell far,
those who are born and those who are yet to be born —

may all beings, without exception, be happy.

The monks went back to the grove.
They practiced what the Buddha had taught.

And the unease...
gradually...
dissolved.

Not because the forest changed.
Not because whatever had been watching them went away.

But because they had changed.

They had stopped moving through the grove as if they were separate from it.
As if they were visitors who did not belong.
As if the forest were something to be afraid of or to guard against.

And instead...
they had opened.

They had let their goodwill move outward like ripples on water —
touching everything,
asking for nothing,
belonging everywhere.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

Now.

There is something the monks may not have fully noticed in that moment —
something that has taken teachers and practitioners centuries to articulate clearly.

Before the Buddha taught them to send Metta outward to all beings...
before he pointed their hearts toward the east and the west and the north and the south...

he told them something else first.

He told them to begin with themselves.

Because here is what the Buddha understood about the nature of goodwill:

You cannot send out from an empty well.

If you have spent years — perhaps your whole life —
withholding kindness from yourself...
if you have learned to treat yourself as the last one
who deserves patience or warmth or the benefit of the doubt...

then the goodwill you send outward
will always be thin.
Always a little brittle.
Always slightly contingent on how things go,
on how people respond,
on whether you are having a good day
or a bad one.

But when you learn to include yourself
in the circle of your own kindness —
when you stop making yourself the exception to your own compassion —

something shifts.

The well fills.

And what flows outward from a full well...
is different from what flows outward from an empty one.

The practice begins here.
With these four phrases.
Perhaps the most important sentences you will ever learn to say to yourself.

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease.

Say them slowly, in your mind, as I say them.

May I be happy.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

May I be healthy.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

May I be safe.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

May I live with ease.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]

You don't need to feel them yet.
You don't need to believe them.
You don't need to resolve any conflict you might have
with the idea of wishing yourself well.

Just let the words land.
Like seeds placed in ground you're not yet sure will receive them.

The rest will come later.

First, a story.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]


[STORY 1 — THE HEALER WHO FORGOT TO HEAL HERSELF]

There was once a healer named Priya who lived in a small village
at the edge of a river that the locals called the Silver River
because of the way it caught the moonlight on clear nights.

Priya had learned her craft from her grandmother,
who had learned it from her grandmother before her,
and so on back through the generations for as long as anyone could remember.

Healing, in Priya's family, was not a profession.
It was more like a calling that found you
rather than something you chose.

Priya had discovered her gift early.
As a young child she had known, without being taught,
which plants in the garden were for which ailments.
She had pressed her small hands against the foreheads of sick animals
and felt a warmth move through her palms
that she could not explain but that seemed, somehow, to help.

By the time she was an adult, word had spread far beyond her village.

People came from three villages away.
From the other side of the mountains.
Some came days on foot to reach her.

She helped them all.

She knew which roots to boil for fevers that came with the summer rains.
She knew which leaves to steep into a tea
for the kind of deep, bone-tired exhaustion
that has nothing to do with lack of sleep.
She knew how to set a broken arm
and how to speak to someone whose spirit seemed to have gone out of them
like a lamp running dry of oil —
which, she had long understood, was its own kind of illness,
and often the most serious kind.

She had a gift with these ones in particular.
The ones who had stopped being able to find a reason
to get out of bed in the morning.
The ones who sat with their hands in their laps
and looked at nothing.

For them, Priya seemed to know exactly what to do.
She would sit across from them
and not immediately try to fix anything.
She would make tea.
She would be quiet.
She would wait until the silence between them became comfortable,
and then she would begin to talk —
about small things at first,
about nothing important,
about the weather or the harvest or a bird she had seen that morning —
until she felt the person across from her begin to loosen slightly,
the way ice does when the air begins to warm.

And then she would ask:
What has gone missing for you?

Not: what is wrong with you.
Not: why can't you just feel better.

What has gone missing.

The question landed differently.
It acknowledged that something real had been lost.
It made space for grief.
And in that space, people began to find the words
for the thing they had been carrying alone.

Priya was gifted at this.
She was patient and warm and unhurried.
She had what the older healers called a quality of presence —
an ability to be so fully with another person
that the person felt, perhaps for the first time in a long while,
that they were not alone.

People left her house lighter.

She went to bed most nights exhausted —
bone-tired in the way that is also, underneath it,
deeply satisfying.
The tiredness of someone who has done something real.

This was how things were for many years.

And then, slowly, almost imperceptibly,
something began to change.

It did not happen all at once.
It happened the way the tide moves —
so gradually that you don't notice it
until you look down and realize the water is up to your knees.

The tiredness remained,
but the satisfaction underneath it began to thin.

She still helped everyone who came.
She still made the tea.
She still asked: what has gone missing for you.

But when she asked it now,
she found herself unable to say what she herself was missing.

She was not even sure she had noticed she was missing something.

She just kept moving.

She rose before the sun most mornings.
She was still seeing patients after dark on most nights.
She had stopped taking her walks by the Silver River —
the walks that had once been her way of refilling,
of hearing her own thoughts again,
of remembering that she was a person and not only a function.

She told herself she was too busy.
She told herself other people needed her more.
She told herself she would rest properly when things slowed down.

Things did not slow down.

One evening in late autumn —
the kind of evening when the light goes early
and the air carries the first real cold of the season —
a woman named Deepa came to visit.

Deepa was Priya's oldest friend.
They had grown up in the same village,
climbed the same trees as children,
sat beside the same river on summer nights watching the water catch the stars.

Deepa had moved to another town after her marriage,
and years had passed between their visits.
But she had heard, from travelers, that Priya was not quite herself.

She had decided to come and see.

She found Priya at the table in the center of her house.

The lamp was burning low.
There were medicines and dried herbs
spread across every surface.
Several open books with pages of notes she had been making.
A cup of tea, long gone cold, sitting forgotten at her elbow.

Priya looked up when Deepa came in
and the expression on her face was something Deepa had never seen there before.

It was not sadness exactly.
It was not illness.
It was more like the look of someone who has been in a room
for so long
they have forgotten there are other rooms.

Deepa pulled up a chair.
She sat down across from Priya.
And she looked at her the way Priya had looked at so many people
across so many years.

Quietly.
Without rushing.
Without immediately trying to fix anything.

Priya noticed the quality of her friend's attention
and something in her went very still.

They sat like that for what felt like a long time.

Finally, Deepa asked:

"When did you last sleep through the whole night?"

Priya opened her mouth to answer
and then realized she could not remember.

"When did you last eat a meal
without thinking about the next person who needed you?"

Priya was quiet.

"When did you last sit somewhere that wasn't this house
and do nothing in particular?
Not rest so you could return to work.
Just... be somewhere, for its own sake?"

Priya looked at the cold cup of tea at her elbow.
She did not answer.

Deepa reached across the table and took her hands.

They were rough. Work-worn. The hands of someone
who used them every day for other people's needs.

"You know every remedy," Deepa said gently.
"You have helped more people in this valley
than anyone can count."

She paused.

"But you have forgotten the one remedy
that keeps all the others working."

Priya looked at her.
Her eyes had filled.
She had not expected to feel this.
She had not known there was anything here to feel.

"Which one?" she said.

"The one you give yourself," Deepa said.

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]

They talked for a long time that night.

Deepa stayed three days.

On the morning of the third day,
before she left,
she sat with Priya by the Silver River —
which Priya had not visited in so long
that she had forgotten how the water sounded.

It sounded like it always had.
Like something that had been moving before she was born
and would keep moving long after she was gone.
Unhurried. Steady. Completely indifferent to urgency.

Deepa said: "The river does not worry about running out."

Priya watched the water.

"I know," she said. "But I'm not a river."

"No," Deepa said. "But you are not a lamp that needs to burn until it goes out either.
You are something in between.
Something that can be refilled.
Something that needs to be refilled."

She was quiet for a moment.

"The question is," Deepa said,
"do you believe that you deserve to be filled
as much as the people you fill?"

Priya did not answer immediately.

The river moved.

After a long time, she said: "I'm not sure I do."

Deepa nodded, as if this was not a surprise.
As if she had known this was the thing.
The real thing, underneath all the busyness and the exhaustion.

"That's where we start, then," Deepa said.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]

Before Deepa left that morning,
she taught Priya something her own teacher had taught her many years ago.

Four phrases.
Simple phrases.
Directed inward.

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease.

Priya said them out loud, standing beside the river,
feeling slightly foolish.

Deepa said: "You don't need to feel them yet.
You just need to say them.
The way you water a seed
long before you see anything growing.
You don't wait until you see the green shoot
to start watering.
You water first.
You trust the process."

Priya said them again.

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease.

This time, something small and unexpected happened.

The tightness in her chest —
the one she had been living with so long
she had stopped noticing it —
loosened.

Just slightly.

Just for a moment.

Like the first small crack of light
at the edge of a long night.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


[REFLECTION 1]

There is something in Priya's story
that many of us recognize.

Maybe not all of it.
Maybe not the healer part,
or the river part,
or the friend who travels to find us.

But that thing underneath it.

The way that care for others can quietly become
a reason we never get around to caring for ourselves.

The way busyness becomes a kind of armor.
The way exhaustion can feel, strangely, safer
than stillness.
Because in stillness there is space to notice
what has gone missing.

And many of us are not ready to notice that.
Not yet.

The Buddha did not teach Metta as a luxury
for people who have taken care of everything else first.

He taught it as a foundation.

A root.

Something that holds the whole tree upright.

When a tree's roots are shallow,
it can look fine for a long time.
It can grow, even.
It can offer shade and fruit and beauty.

But the first strong wind that comes...
reveals the truth.

And the roots of our ability to care for others...
run through our relationship with ourselves.

Not in a selfish way.
Not in the way our culture sometimes fears
when it hears the word "self-care" —
as if caring for yourself necessarily comes at the expense of caring for others.

But in the way a lamp that is properly fueled
can light a room.
And a lamp that has run dry
can light nothing.

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease.

These words are not self-indulgence.

They are the river filling.
They are the lamp being refueled.
They are the root growing deeper
so the tree can offer more —
not less —
to everything around it.

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]


[STORY 2 — THE OLD MONK AND THE MIRROR]

There is a story told in Zen circles
about a teacher named Fujiwara
who had spent forty-two years in practice.

He had meditated in mountain monasteries
where the winters were brutal and the silence was total.
He had studied with three teachers,
each of whom had given him a different piece of the same truth.
He had read the ancient texts until his eyes began to fail him,
and even then he continued,
running his fingers along the page
in the dim light of early morning.

He was known throughout his region
as a teacher of great patience and clarity.

Students came from far away to sit with him.

He was especially known for his ability to help people
with the practice of Metta —
of loving-kindness.

He could guide a student toward genuine warmth
for even the most difficult people in their lives.
He had a gift for dissolving the hardness
that people built around old wounds.

One of his younger students —
a woman named Akemi who had come to the monastery
after many years of working in the city —
came to him one afternoon in a state of frustration.

She had been practicing Metta for several months.
She had made what she felt was genuine progress
with some of the harder directions —
she had found a way to send goodwill, imperfectly but honestly,
to a former colleague who had hurt her professionally.
She had found she could extend warmth, most days,
to strangers she encountered.

But there was one direction where she kept hitting a wall.

"I cannot do it toward myself," she told Fujiwara.
"Every time I try, something in me resists.
The words feel hollow.
They feel like I'm reciting something
I don't believe."

Fujiwara listened to her.
He was quiet for a moment.

Then he said: "Come with me."

He led her to a storage room at the back of the monastery —
a small, dim room where old cushions and broken furniture were kept,
waiting to be repaired or disposed of.

In the corner of the room,
half covered by a cloth,
was an old mirror.

The glass was slightly clouded with age.
The frame was simple, dark wood, unadorned.

Fujiwara pulled the cloth away.

"Sit here," he said, placing a cushion in front of it.

Akemi sat.

She saw herself in the old glass.
Her own face, a little blurred at the edges by age and cloud.
Her own eyes, looking back at her.

"Now," Fujiwara said quietly.
"Look at the face in the mirror.
And try to look at it the way you would look at someone you loved."

He left her there.

[PAUSE — 5 seconds]

Akemi tried.

She looked at her own face.
And immediately the voice started.

Not the words of Metta.
A different voice.
The other one.
The familiar one.

It pointed out the lines around her eyes.
The way her mouth sat when it was at rest.
The slight asymmetry in her face
that she had noticed at seventeen and never stopped noticing since.
The way she looked when she was tired,
which she was, often.

She tried to look past all of that.
She tried to find the warmth
that came naturally when she looked at people she loved.

She could not find it.

When she looked at her mother, or her closest friend,
or even a stranger who reminded her of someone vulnerable —
the warmth was there.
Immediate.
Real.

But when she looked at her own face,
the warmth became intellectual.
An idea of warmth. Not warmth itself.
I should feel kind toward this person.
But actually feeling it... was another matter.

She sat there for a long time.

The light in the small room changed.
The monastery sounds moved through their rhythms around her.
Meals. Walking meditation. The bell for evening practice.

She was still sitting.

On the second day, Fujiwara came to check on her.

He found her still in front of the mirror.

"How is it?" he asked.

She shook her head slowly.
"I can do it for a moment.
And then the judgment comes back.
And when the judgment comes, I can't find the warmth again."

Fujiwara sat down beside her.

"When you look at a person you love," he said,
"and they have made a mistake —
how do you look at them?"

Akemi thought. "I... still love them.
The mistake doesn't take the love away."

"Why?"

"Because I know who they are," she said.
"The mistake is just a moment.
It's not the whole person."

Fujiwara nodded.

"And when you look at yourself
and you see your mistakes...
do you offer the same understanding?"

She looked at herself in the old glass.
Her face, a little blurred.
Her own eyes.

"No," she said.

"For yourself," Fujiwara said gently,
"the mistake is the person.
Isn't it.

When you look in that mirror,
you don't see all the moments of your life —
the kindness, the effort, the times you got it right,
the ways you have grown,
the struggles you endured and survived.

You see the moments you fell short.
And you let those moments be the whole story."

Akemi was quiet.

He was right.
She could feel that he was right
in the part of her that knew things before she had words for them.

"But how do you change that?" she said.
"You can't just decide to see yourself differently."

"No," he said.
"You practice.
You do what you would do with any skill you do not have yet.
You begin where you are.
You do it imperfectly.
You do it again.

Not because you feel it yet.
Because you intend to.
Because intention, practiced with patience,
eventually becomes feeling."

He stood.

"You came to me saying you could not do Metta toward yourself," he said.
"But you have been sitting in front of that mirror for two days.
That is not someone who cannot do it.
That is someone who is beginning."

[PAUSE — 6 seconds]

On the third day, something shifted.

Akemi did not know why it happened on the third day and not the first.
These things rarely have explanations.

She was sitting, as before,
in front of the old clouded mirror.

And she looked at herself.

And instead of beginning with the judgment —
instead of the inventory of shortcomings
that had become so automatic she barely noticed it —

she looked at her eyes.

Just her eyes.

And she had a thought she had not had before.

This person has tried very hard.

Not: this person has succeeded.
Not: this person is without flaw.
Not: this person deserves special recognition.

Just:

This person has tried very hard.
For a long time.
In difficult circumstances.
With imperfect tools.

And that is... enough.
That is something.
That deserves at least a moment of acknowledgment.

She looked at her own eyes in the old glass.

And for the first time in perhaps her entire life,
she looked at herself
without immediately looking for what was wrong.

She was just... there.
A person.
Tired and trying and real.

And something in her opened.

Just slightly.
Just a crack.

Enough to feel, for one breath,
what it might be like to be her own friend.

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease.

She said them.

And this time,
they did not feel hollow.

[PAUSE — 8 seconds]


[REFLECTION 2]

What Akemi discovered in that storage room
is something that many practitioners discover
after months or years of trying.

That the resistance to self-compassion
is not weakness.

It is not selfishness.
It is not egotism hiding as modesty.

It is, in fact, a learned pattern.

Most of us received messages very early in our lives —
from parents, from schools, from culture, from religion —
that humility meant thinking less of yourself.
That self-criticism was the same thing as self-improvement.
That to be a good person meant to put yourself last.

And so we practiced that.

We practiced and practiced
until self-judgment became as automatic as breathing.
Until the critical voice inside
felt like the voice of conscience itself —
like the part of us that keeps us honest.

The Buddha taught that this is a confusion.

Self-criticism is not the same as self-awareness.
Harshness is not the same as honesty.
Putting yourself last does not make you more caring toward others.
It makes you more depleted.
And a depleted person cannot truly care for anyone.

The mirror in Akemi's story
is not just a piece of glass in a storage room.

It is the practice itself.

The practice of being willing to look.
Of sitting with what we see
long enough to move past the first wave of judgment.

Long enough to notice:

This person has tried.
This person is real.
This person deserves what I would offer, without hesitation,
to anyone else I loved.

May I be happy.
May I be healthy.
May I be safe.
May I live with ease.

Tonight, as you rest,
let these words be less like an affirmation
and more like a small act of courage.

The courage of looking at yourself
without immediately looking for what is wrong.

The courage of saying:
I am here.
I am trying.
And that is enough.

For now.
For tonight.

That is enough.

[PAUSE — 10 seconds]

Let us pause here together for a moment.

Take a slow, deep breath.

Let it fill you from the bottom up —
from the belly first,
then the chest,
then all the way to the collarbone.

Hold it for just a moment.

And then... let it go.

Feel your body sink a little more deeply
into whatever is beneath you.

The practice we have been exploring tonight
is not about thinking the right things.

It is about being willing to stay.
To sit in front of the mirror.
To say the words even when they feel hollow.
To trust that the feeling follows the practice
rather than the other way around.

You have been doing that tonight.

That matters.
